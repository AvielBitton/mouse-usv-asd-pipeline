Analysis

1. TabPFN Achieves Higher Overall Accuracy (Random Split)

With random split, TabPFN outperforms XGBoost in raw accuracy on both data sources:

However, this improvement comes at a significant cost: TabPFN almost completely ignores the minority HET class (27% and 15% recall respectively), predicting nearly everything as WT. The higher accuracy reflects the class imbalance (70–75% WT), not better disease detection.

2. XGBoost Detects Disease Far Better (HET Recall)

Across all configurations, XGBoost dramatically outperforms TabPFN on HET recall — the metric that matters most for disease screening:

XGBoost’s compute_sample_weight(balanced) and scale_pos_weight=0.8 actively compensate for class imbalance, pushing the model to detect HET mice even at the cost of WT false positives. TabPFN has no such mechanism and defaults to predicting the majority class.

3. The Accuracy–Sensitivity Tradeoff

The two models occupy opposite ends of the precision–recall tradeoff:

  • XGBoost: High HET recall (catches 90–97% of disease cases), low WT recall (many false alarms) → sensitive screener
  • TabPFN: High WT recall (94–97%), near-zero HET recall (misses 73–85% of disease cases) → majority-class predictor

For a disease detection task, missing HET mice is far worse than false positives. XGBoost’s bias toward sensitivity is the correct strategy.

4. Group Split Performance

Both models degrade under group-aware split, but TabPFN shows an interesting pattern on external data:

XGBoost: Random → GroupTabPFN: Random → Group
Pipeline: Accuracy drop−8.3%−19.7%
External: Accuracy drop−14.6%−10.6%

TabPFN’s smaller drop on external data with group split (65.6% vs XGBoost’s 56.1%) suggests it may generalize better to unseen mice when given more data. However, this comes with catastrophically low HET detection (17% recall), making it unusable for screening.

5. Why TabPFN Fails on HET Detection

  • No class balancing: TabPFN v1 does not accept sample_weight. With 70–76% of the data being WT, the model learns to predict the majority class.
  • Prior mismatch: TabPFN was pre-trained on synthetic balanced datasets. The strong class imbalance in this dataset (24–30% HET) is outside its training distribution.
  • Small effective signal: The discriminative acoustic features contribute only 1–3% each to classification. TabPFN’s attention mechanism may not amplify these weak signals as effectively as XGBoost’s boosting, especially without explicit class weighting.

TabPFN Cannot Detect Disease

Despite higher overall accuracy in most configurations, TabPFN misses 73–85% of HET (ASD-model) mice in random split. For a disease screening task, this makes it unsuitable as a standalone classifier. The accuracy improvement is an artifact of predicting the majority class (WT), not better disease detection.

XGBoost Remains the Best Option

XGBoost with class balancing detects 90–97% of HET mice (random split) and 70–90% (group split), making it the only viable model for ASD screening. Its lower overall accuracy reflects the deliberate tradeoff: catching disease cases matters more than avoiding false alarms.

Recommendations

Keep XGBoost as the primary classifier. TabPFN v1 is not competitive for this imbalanced disease detection task.

  • TabPFN v2 (requires Python 3.9+) supports larger datasets and may handle imbalance better — worth revisiting when upgrading the Python environment.
  • Ensemble approach: combining XGBoost’s sensitivity with TabPFN’s specificity could yield a better balanced classifier.
  • CatBoost / LightGBM are natural next candidates — they support class weighting natively and may outperform XGBoost on this data.
  • Improve acoustic features: both models struggle because mother_gen dominates (~50% importance). Stronger syllable-level features would benefit any model.
